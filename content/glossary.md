title: Glossary
---

## Break even
Getting back to the money you started with, so your total result is zero: no gain and no loss.

Costs count too: buying and later selling at the same price can still leave a loss after fees. Break even is about your net result, not merely the price returning to your entry.

**Hypothetical example.** You pay $100 plus $1 in fees. Selling later for $100 with another $1 fee leaves you $2 down, not at break even.

## Cross margin
A setting where your whole account balance backs a leveraged trade. If the trade goes badly, it can use up everything, not just the margin you put in.

Several positions can draw on the same eligible collateral. This can delay one position's liquidation, but also lets its losses threaten money supporting other positions. Funds outside that shared margin pool are a separate matter.

**Hypothetical example.** One trade starts with $50 of margin, but a worsening loss can consume more of the $500 shared pool.

## Drawdown
How far your account (or a price) has fallen from its last high point. A 20% drawdown means you're 20% below your peak.

The reference point is the previous peak, not necessarily your starting balance. Recovering from a drawdown requires a larger percentage gain than the percentage lost because the gain starts from a smaller amount.

**Hypothetical example.** An account falls from $1,000 to $800: a 20% drawdown. It then needs to gain 25% on $800 to return to $1,000.

## Expectancy
What a strategy makes or loses on average per trade over many trades. It combines how often you win with how much you win or lose each time.

Multiply the proportion of winners by the average win, then subtract the proportion of losers multiplied by the average loss. Use results after costs. An apparent advantage in a small sample may be chance.

**Hypothetical example.** Winning $30 on 40% of trades and losing $10 on 60% gives an average of $6 per trade before costs—not a guaranteed $6 on each trade.

## Hypothetical example
A made-up example used to show how something works. The numbers are invented, but the maths is real.

Its purpose is to make a mechanism easier to follow without pretending to report a real trade or current market data. Changing the assumptions can change the outcome, so the example is an illustration rather than evidence that a strategy works.

For instance, an invented account can demonstrate leverage mathematically. It cannot establish the returns a real trader should expect.

## Index
A number summarizing a chosen set of investments or measurements. A stock index, such as the S&P 500, tracks the performance of a group of companies.

An index combines selected components according to a formula. Some track investment prices; others summarize conditions such as sentiment. The selection and weighting determine what the number represents, so an index is not the whole market.

**Hypothetical example.** A stock index can rise because its largest members rise even while many smaller members fall.

## Isolated margin
An arrangement that assigns a separate amount of collateral to a leveraged position instead of sharing a margin pool across positions.

Collateral is assigned to a particular position rather than shared across all positions. This helps separate that trade's margin risk, but you must check the venue's rules, fees and whether automatic margin additions are enabled.

**Hypothetical example.** You allocate $50 to one isolated position. Adding another $30 later increases the collateral exposed; the original $50 is no longer the whole amount at risk.

## Leverage
Taking market exposure larger than the money you put up, through borrowing or a margin-based contract. It magnifies gains and losses relative to that money.

Price changes affect the full position, not just the money you put up. That is why a modest market move can produce a large change in your own capital. Fees and funding can add to the effect.

**Hypothetical example.** With $100 supporting $1,000 of exposure, a 5% adverse move loses about $50 before costs: half your $100.

## Liquidation
When an exchange forcibly closes a leveraged position because its collateral no longer meets the required margin. Much or all of the money supporting that position can be lost.

The platform acts to prevent the position's losses from exceeding its permitted collateral cushion. This is different from a stop loss you chose yourself. Liquidation rules depend on the contract and venue, and closure can happen before the margin reaches zero.

**Hypothetical example.** A leveraged long loses value as price falls. If its collateral becomes insufficient, the exchange closes it without waiting for your permission.

## Liquidation price
The reference price at which a platform expects to forcibly close a leveraged position if its margin becomes insufficient.

It depends on leverage, collateral, maintenance-margin requirements and sometimes other positions. The venue may use a mark price rather than the last trade shown on your chart. Adding margin or changing the position can move the threshold.

For a long, the dangerous direction is downward; for a short, upward. A liquidation price is a forced-closure boundary, not a sensible substitute for a risk plan.

## Liquidity
How easily you can buy or sell something without moving its price much. Low liquidity means wider gaps between buy and sell prices.

What matters is the quantity of buying and selling available near the current price, not just how popular an asset looks. A large order may have to accept progressively worse prices when little is available.

**Hypothetical example.** Only a few units are offered at $10. Buying many more units requires paying $10.20 and $10.50 too, moving the average purchase price higher.

## Margin
The money you put down to open a leveraged trade, like a deposit. Leverage lets the trade be bigger than this amount.

Margin is collateral supporting the exposure, not the size of the trade and not necessarily a fee. Initial margin lets you open the position; maintenance margin is what must remain to keep it open under the venue's rules.

**Hypothetical example.** A $1,000 position requires $100 initially. Its gains and losses follow the $1,000 exposure, while the $100 acts as collateral.

## Position size
The total amount of an asset a position represents, expressed as units or market value. It is a main input in controlling trade risk.

Position size can mean a number of units or their total market value. It is different from margin and from the loss you intend to accept. The same percentage price move produces a bigger cash result on a bigger position.

**Hypothetical example.** A 5% fall on a $200 position loses $10; on a $2,000 position it loses $100, before costs.

## Risk per trade
The amount you're willing to lose on one trade if it goes wrong, often set as a small percentage of your account.

This is the planned loss if your exit is reached, not the amount spent buying the position. It depends on position size and the distance to that exit. Actual losses can exceed the plan through gaps, slippage or execution failure.

**Hypothetical example.** A $500 position with an intended exit 2% below entry has about $10 of planned price risk before costs.

## S&P 500
An index of about 500 large companies listed in the United States. It's often used as a quick read on the US stock market.

The companies do not contribute equally: larger eligible companies generally carry more weight. The index is a reference for a large part of US equities, not every stock or the entire economy. Buying an index fund is different from buying the index itself.

**Hypothetical example.** Strong gains in a few heavyweight companies can outweigh small declines across many other members.

## Stop loss
An order intended to close a position when the price moves against it by a chosen amount. It helps limit losses, but gaps, slippage or an unfilled stop-limit order can produce a larger loss than planned.

The trigger tells the system when to activate your exit. What happens next depends on the order type: a stop-market prioritizes execution, while a stop-limit restricts the acceptable price but may not execute. Neither guarantees an exact final loss.

**Hypothetical example.** A long bought at $100 has a stop at $95. A sudden gap to $90 may prevent an exit near $95.

## Volatility
How much and how fast a price moves up and down. High volatility means bigger swings in both directions.

It describes the size and variability of price changes, not whether those changes are good or bad. An asset can be volatile while moving sideways, and a steady decline can be less volatile than violent alternating rises and falls.

**Hypothetical example.** One asset moves 1% most days; another regularly swings 8%. Equal position sizes expose you to very different price movement.

## Win rate
The share of your trades that make money. A high win rate alone doesn't mean a strategy is profitable.

Count winning trades and divide by the total number of completed trades, using a consistent definition of a win. You also need average gains, average losses and costs to judge the overall result.

**Hypothetical example.** Nine trades win $1 each and one loses $20. The win rate is 90%, yet the total result is a loss of $11 before costs.

## Account
The money you've set aside for trading. Only money you can afford to lose belongs here, not savings or bills.

Here, account means your trading capital or bankroll, not merely a login. Its size is the reference for position risk, drawdown and performance. Deposits and withdrawals need to be separated from trading gains and losses.

**Hypothetical example.** Adding $500 to a $1,000 account raises the balance to $1,500, but that increase is a deposit—not a 50% trading return.

## Break-even win rate
The share of trades you need to win just to end up at zero, given your reward to risk. At 2:1 it's about 33%.

Before costs, divide the average loss by the average win plus the average loss. This calculation uses what trades actually win or lose, not just the attractive target drawn before entry. Fees raise the required success rate.

**Hypothetical example.** If winners average $20 and losers $10, roughly one winner in three covers the losses before costs; equal results are not guaranteed.

## Reward to risk
How much a trade could make compared with how much it could lose. Risking $10 to make $20 is 2:1.

Compare the potential profit at your intended exit with the planned loss at your invalidation point. An attractive ratio says nothing by itself about how likely either outcome is. Actual results can differ when exits are partial or execution is poor.

**Hypothetical example.** A trade targeting $30 while planning to risk $10 offers 3:1, but repeated failed attempts can still make it unprofitable.

## Slippage
The gap between the price you asked for and the price you actually got. It grows when prices move fast or few people are trading.

Your order meets the prices available when it executes, which may differ from what you saw a moment earlier. Large orders, rapid changes and thin order books can increase the difference. Slippage can occasionally be favourable too.

**Hypothetical example.** You expect to buy at $10, but the available offers move and your average fill is $10.08. The extra $0.08 per unit is adverse slippage.

## Stop distance
How far the price can move against you, from your entry to your stop, usually as a percentage.

For a long, compare the entry price with the lower planned stop; for a short, with the higher planned stop. A wider distance means more potential loss for the same position size, so position size and stop distance must be considered together.

**Hypothetical example.** Entry at $100 and a stop at $95 create a 5% distance, not a $5 total account loss.

## Stop limit order
An order that creates a limit order after a specified trigger price is reached. It controls acceptable execution prices but may not fill.

It combines a trigger price with a limit price. Reaching the trigger creates a limit order, but someone must still trade at the limit or better. A fast move can leave your position open while losses continue.

**Hypothetical example.** A sell stop triggers at $95 with a $94 limit. If the market jumps straight to $90, the sell order may remain unfilled.

## Stop market order
An order that creates a market order to close a position once its trigger is reached. It prioritizes execution rather than an exact price.

Reaching the trigger creates a market order to close the position. That usually makes execution more likely than with a stop-limit, but the fill depends on available liquidity. For a short, the closing order is a buy rather than a sell.

**Hypothetical example.** A sell stop activates at $95, but a fast fall means the actual average exit is $93.

## Target
The price where you plan to take your profit, chosen before you enter the trade.

A target is an intended exit, not a prediction or a promise that price will reach it. It helps compare possible reward with risk and plan whether to close all or part of a position. The plan still needs a response if the target is never reached.

**Hypothetical example.** Buying at $100 with a $110 target describes a hoped-for 10% price gain, not a guaranteed return.

## Funding rate
A regular fee on many crypto perpetual futures, paid between traders every few hours while a position is open. It can be paid or received.

Funding helps keep a perpetual contract's price near its spot reference. On many venues, positive funding means longs pay shorts; negative funding reverses the direction. The rate, interval and calculation depend on the contract.

**Hypothetical example.** Funding of 0.01% on $10,000 of notional exposure is about $1 for that interval—not 0.01% of a smaller margin deposit.

## Losing streak
A run of losing trades in a row. Normal even for good strategies, which is why risk per trade is kept small.

The sequence matters because several losses can arrive before later gains, even when long-run results look positive. A streak does not make a winner due, and a planned strategy still needs limits for unusually bad runs.

**Hypothetical example.** Five successive losses of $10 remove $50. Doubling the next stake does not make the next trade more likely to win.

## Maker and taker fees
Two fee rates many exchanges use. A maker order waits in the order book; a taker order fills straight away. Maker fees are often lower.

The classification depends on what your order actually does. A limit order that immediately crosses available orders can be a taker; a resting order that adds liquidity can be a maker. Compare total costs, including spread and funding, not one advertised fee.

**Hypothetical example.** At a 0.1% fee, a $1,000 fill costs $1; opening and closing creates two fills.

## Rug pull
A scam where the people behind a token hype it, then sell everything or drain its funds and disappear.

Losses can come from removed trading liquidity, abusive token controls or insiders dumping a large allocation. Not every price crash is a rug pull, but a polished website or enthusiastic community does not establish safety.

**Hypothetical example.** Buyers can purchase a token, but its creators remove the pool funds needed to sell it back. A displayed price may remain even when meaningful selling is impossible.

## Seed phrase
A wallet's recovery words, often 12 or 24, which can restore access to its controlling keys. Treat them as secret access to the funds, not ordinary account information.

The words can recreate the wallet's controlling keys. They are not the same as an ordinary account password: someone with the phrase may move the funds without your device or approval. A genuine support conversation does not require you to reveal it.

Keep recovery words offline and private. Entering them into an unsolicited website can hand over control even if you never approve a transaction afterward.

## Spread
The gap between the highest price a buyer will pay and the lowest price a seller will accept.

Best bid is the highest displayed buying offer; best ask is the lowest selling offer. Their difference is a trading cost when you cross between them. It can widen during uncertainty or when there are fewer available orders.

**Hypothetical example.** Bid is $9.90 and ask is $10. Immediately buying then selling leaves about a $0.10 loss per unit before fees.

## Two-factor authentication (2FA)
A second check when you log in, such as a code from an app on your phone, on top of your password.

The second factor makes a stolen password less useful, but it is not complete protection. Codes can be phished, sessions stolen and recovery methods abused. Authenticator apps and security keys differ from SMS in how they work and the risks they reduce.

A request to read out a login code can be an attempt to enter your account—not a routine support check.

## Ask
The lowest price any seller is currently willing to accept. If you buy straight away, you usually pay the ask.

It refers to an available selling offer, not the price every seller will accept. The quantity offered matters: buying more than is available at the best ask can fill the remainder at higher asks. Quotes can also change before your order arrives.

**Hypothetical example.** Two units are offered at $10 and the next three at $10.20. Buying five averages more than $10.

## Bid
The highest price any buyer is currently offering. If you sell straight away, you usually get the bid.

It refers to an available buying offer, not a guaranteed exit price for an unlimited amount. A large sale may exhaust the best bid and continue into lower bids. This is one reason a displayed last price may differ from what you can sell for.

**Hypothetical example.** Buyers bid for only one unit at $10; selling ten units may require accepting lower prices too.

## Candlestick
A chart shape that shows four prices for one period: open, high, low and close. Green usually means it closed higher, red lower.

The body joins opening and closing prices; the thin wicks show the period's highest and lowest prices. Colour usually compares close with open, not with every earlier trade. A candle still forming can change shape or colour before its period ends.

**Hypothetical example.** A day opens at $100, reaches $110 and closes at $98. It is red despite trading higher during the day.

## Limit order
An order to buy or sell only at your chosen price or better. It may execute immediately or wait for a match, and may never fill.

A buy limit sets the highest price you will accept; a sell limit sets the lowest. The trade still needs available counterparties, and other orders can be ahead of you. Touching the price on a chart does not guarantee your whole order fills.

**Hypothetical example.** A buy limit at $10 will not deliberately pay $10.20, but it may remain unfilled if sellers never reach $10.

## Market order
An order to buy or sell straight away at the best price available.

It prioritizes executing against current available orders rather than controlling an exact price. One large market order can fill across several price levels. During fast moves, the final average can differ substantially from the last quoted price.

**Hypothetical example.** A market buy fills part at $10 and part at $10.30. The order executes, but not entirely at the first displayed price.

## Order book
A trading venue's displayed list of buy and sell orders, showing their prices and quantities. It helps show available trading interest near the current price.

Each entry shows a price and available quantity on that venue. Buyers and sellers may add, cancel or change orders, so the book is a changing snapshot—not a commitment that all displayed liquidity will stay. It also does not show every kind of hidden or off-venue interest.

A large visible buying order can disappear before price reaches it; it is not guaranteed support.

## Range
When a price moves sideways between a rough floor and a rough ceiling, without a clear trend.

The boundaries are approximate areas where advances or declines have repeatedly stalled. A range is identified over a chosen timeframe and can contain many smaller trends. It ends only when price leaves it in a meaningful way, and apparent escapes can fail.

**Hypothetical example.** Repeated movement between $90 and $100 describes a range, not proof that the next touch of $90 will rebound.

## Resistance
A price area where rises have stopped before, because sellers stepped in. An area to watch, not a promise.

Previous buyers may sell when price returns to their entry, while other traders take profits or open shorts. These actions can create selling interest, but the exact participants are usually unknown. Enough buying can still push through the area.

**Hypothetical example.** Several rallies stop around $100. That makes $100 worth observing, not a ceiling the market is unable to cross.

## Support
A price area where falls have stopped before, because buyers stepped in. An area to watch, not a promise.

Some participants may see value there, close short positions or place planned buy orders. Their interest can slow a decline, but it can also vanish or be overwhelmed. Support is usually a zone rather than one exact price.

**Hypothetical example.** Falls repeatedly pause around $100. If buyers stop stepping in, price can fall through that same area rather than bounce again.

## Timeframe
How much time each candle on a chart covers, such as one hour or one day.

Changing it groups the same trading activity into different-sized periods. A daily candle contains many hourly candles, so the daily trend can be falling while the last few hours rise. Your holding period and the chart's candle period are related but not identical.

**Hypothetical example.** A four-hour recovery can sit inside a week that still closes lower.

## Trend
A general direction over time. An uptrend makes higher highs and higher lows; a downtrend makes lower highs and lower lows.

It describes a pattern across several price swings, not every individual move. Pullbacks can occur inside an uptrend and bounces inside a downtrend. Which swings matter depends on the timeframe, so two charts can show different trends without either being wrong.

**Hypothetical example.** Successive highs of $100, $110 and $120 suggest upward structure even if price briefly falls between them.

## Circulating supply
An estimate of how many token units are currently circulating in the market, rather than the full amount that might eventually exist.

For tokens, this usually aims to describe the supply currently available in public circulation rather than all units that may ever exist. Locked allocations and provider methods can affect the figure. For stocks, shares outstanding and free float are distinct measures, so do not treat them as interchangeable.

**Hypothetical example.** A token has 100 million units planned, but only 20 million currently circulating.

## Economic calendar
A free list of upcoming scheduled announcements, such as interest rate decisions and inflation figures.

It normally shows the release time, the previous reading and a forecast or consensus estimate. Markets may react to the surprise relative to expectations, revisions or details—not just whether the headline number looks large. Unscheduled events are not covered by a calendar.

**Hypothetical example.** Inflation falls, but by less than expected. The market reaction can differ from a simple 'lower inflation is good' story.

## Fully diluted value (FDV)
A token's price multiplied by the supply used for full dilution. It estimates value if that full supply were in circulation at the same price.

FDV imagines more supply valued at today's marginal price; it is not money already invested or a guarantee that all units could be sold at that price. Providers may use maximum or total supply, so check the definition before comparing projects.

**Hypothetical example.** At $2 with 100 million fully diluted units, FDV is $200 million even if far fewer units circulate.

## Futures
Contracts to buy or sell something at a set price on a future date. Many people trade commodities this way.

A futures position follows a contract with its own settlement, expiry and margin rules. Some settle in cash rather than by delivering the asset. Many traders close their position before expiry; perpetual contracts are a related product without the same fixed expiration.

**Hypothetical example.** Holding a contract linked to an asset's price does not necessarily mean you own that asset.

## Gap
When a price opens far from where it last closed, skipping the prices in between. Common after news while a market is closed.

The next available trade can be far beyond an intended entry or stop. In continuously traded markets, a visible gap may also reflect missing data or sparse trading, so check the chart and venue. A gap does not have to be filled later.

**Hypothetical example.** A stock closes at $100 and next opens at $90. A stop near $95 cannot create trades at prices the market skipped.

## Hype cycle
The rise and fall of attention around a story in the market, from almost nobody talking about it, to everybody, and back.

Attention can accelerate prices as more people discover a story, then fade as expectations become difficult to meet. Strong publicity does not establish a profitable business or a lasting token use case. The peak in attention can arrive before obvious bad news.

**Hypothetical example.** A theme dominates social media, but new buyers slow down while early holders sell into the excitement.

## Market cap
The price multiplied by the relevant supply: typically circulating supply for a token or shares outstanding for a company. It is a market valuation, not a bank balance.

It multiplies a price observed at the margin by the relevant supply. It does not measure all cash that entered the asset, nor the amount owners could receive if they sold together. Small trades can reprice the displayed value of a much larger supply.

**Hypothetical example.** One million circulating tokens priced at $2 produce a $2 million market cap, not proof that $2 million was invested.

## Narrative
A story the market is excited about that ties a group of coins or stocks together, such as "AI tokens".

The story can attract attention and link assets that otherwise differ greatly. Price strength across the group may suggest interest, but does not prove fresh money flowed from a particular other sector. Evidence about adoption, revenues or trading conditions still matters.

**Hypothetical example.** Several 'AI' tokens rise together even though their actual products, liquidity and risks are very different.

## Priced in
When expectations about news have already influenced trading and prices before the announcement. Confirmation may then matter less than an unexpected change.

Prices reflect the expectations and positions of participants, not one perfectly informed collective mind. An announcement can still matter if it changes the expected path, reveals new details or differs from the consensus. 'Priced in' is often an interpretation, not something directly observable.

**Hypothetical example.** A widely expected rate cut happens, but prices fall because the accompanying message implies fewer future cuts.

## Unlock
When coins that were held back, often for a team or early investors, are released and can be sold.

An unlock changes which holders are allowed to transfer or sell certain tokens. It can increase potential selling supply, but unlocked holders may choose not to sell. Timing, recipient allocations, existing liquidity and what traders already expected all affect the impact.

**Hypothetical example.** Ten million tokens become transferable on a date; that does not mean ten million tokens must be sold that day.

## Volume
How much was traded in a period, shown as bars along the bottom of most charts.

Trading volume measures turnover: every completed trade has both a buyer and a seller. It does not by itself show net money entering an asset. Data can be reported in units or currency value and can differ across venues or include unreliable activity.

**Hypothetical example.** The same token traded repeatedly can generate high volume without a comparable increase in new holders.

## Backtest
Checking how your trading rules would have done on past charts. Useful, but easy to fool yourself with hindsight.

Rules must be precise enough to apply consistently, including entries, exits, costs and position sizing. A test can look excellent if it uses future information or if settings were repeatedly tuned to the same history. Testing on different data helps reveal that problem.

A backtest is a historical experiment, not a record of trades you actually executed or a guarantee of future performance.

## Paper trading
Following your trading rules in real time with pretend money, often in a demo account.

It lets you practise placing orders, following rules and recording decisions without exposing real capital. Demo fills, fees and emotions may differ from real conditions, so success in simulation is not proof that the same results will transfer.

**Hypothetical example.** A demo order fills instantly at a convenient price, while a real order of that size might suffer slippage or remain partly unfilled.

## R (R-multiple)
A trade's result measured in units of the risk you planned. Risk $20 and make $40, that's +2R. Lose $20, that's −1R.

Define one R from the original planned risk, then express the result relative to that amount. This makes differently sized trades easier to compare. Keep the original reference consistent; moving it afterward can disguise how much risk was really taken.

**Hypothetical example.** Planned risk is $20. A $30 net profit is +1.5R; a $40 loss is −2R, showing the loss exceeded the plan.

## Sample size
The number of observations used to judge a result or claim, such as completed trades. A small sample can give a misleading impression of how repeatable an outcome is.

A few observations can be dominated by luck or one unusual event. More observations usually help estimate a pattern, but only if they are relevant and reasonably comparable. Thousands of overlapping or selectively chosen examples do not automatically provide strong evidence.

**Hypothetical example.** Three winning trades cannot establish a reliable 100% win rate or show how the approach handles a losing period.

## Setup
The specific situation you're looking for before you'll consider a trade, written plainly in your plan.

It describes observable conditions that make an idea worth considering, rather than an emotional urge or a guarantee. A complete trade still needs a trigger, an invalidation point, sizing and an exit plan. Different traders may define different setups on the same chart.

**Hypothetical example.** 'Price returns to a previously tested area' is a condition; 'it feels ready to pump' is not a measurable setup.

## Trade plan
Your trading system on one page: what you trade, when you get in and out, and how much you risk.

A usable plan says what must happen before entry, what would invalidate the idea, how much capital is exposed and how exits are handled. It should also state when not to trade. Writing it before the outcome reduces the temptation to invent a new reason afterward.

A plan manages decisions under uncertainty; it does not remove uncertainty or turn an untested idea into a good strategy.

## Trading journal
A record of every trade: the plan before, what happened, and whether you followed your rules.

Separate the decision made before the trade from what you learned afterward. Recording the setup, size, intended exit, costs and emotional state helps reveal repeated behaviour. A profitable outcome can still come from breaking rules; a planned trade can still lose.

A useful review asks both 'What was the result?' and 'Did I follow a process worth repeating?'

## Trading system
A written set of rules for what you trade, when you get in, when you get out and how much you risk.

A system combines selection, execution, sizing, exits and review into repeatable decisions. Its rules need enough clarity that you can tell whether you followed them. A collection of indicators is not a complete system if it leaves risk and exits undefined.

**Hypothetical example.** Two people use the same chart signal but different position sizes; their account outcomes can be radically different.

## Variance
The normal ups and downs in results that come from chance, even with a good system.

Even with unchanged rules, the order and size of wins and losses fluctuate. Variance helps explain why short-term results can differ from a long-run average, but it is not an excuse to dismiss every loss as bad luck. The underlying approach may also change or fail.

**Hypothetical example.** Two sequences of the same winning and losing trades can produce very different emotional journeys.

## Daily loss limit
The most you allow yourself to lose in one day. Reach it and you stop trading until tomorrow.

The limit is a pre-agreed stopping condition, not a target to lose or a reason to keep trading until you reach it. Define whether it uses realized losses, open losses or both, and allow for costs and execution risk.

**Hypothetical example.** A $30 daily limit is reached after several losses. A new attractive-looking setup does not automatically switch the rule off.

## FOMO
Fear of missing out: the anxious urge to jump into something because it's moving and others seem to be profiting.

The feeling comes from comparing yourself with a move or other people's apparent profits. It can push you to enter without checking price, risk or your original conditions. Social feeds usually show selected successes rather than everyone's complete record.

**Hypothetical example.** You planned to wait, but buy after a sudden rise because 'everyone is making money'. The emotion has replaced the entry rule.

## Overconfidence
Believing you're more skilled, or your next trades more certain, than the evidence shows. Common after a winning streak.

It can show up as larger positions, less checking or certainty after a few favourable outcomes. Skill may be real, but the evidence needs to separate repeatable decisions from luck and supportive market conditions. Confidence is not the same as a high probability of success.

**Hypothetical example.** Three wins persuade someone to triple their next position even though the strategy and evidence have not changed.

## Revenge trading
Taking a new trade mainly to win back a loss, rather than because it fits your plan.

The previous loss becomes the reason for the next decision. That can lead to rushed entries, excessive size or abandoning limits because getting back to the old balance feels urgent. The market does not know what you lost and owes no recovery.

**Hypothetical example.** After losing $20, someone risks $80 on an unplanned trade just to 'make it back quickly'.

## Tilt
A state where frustration or anger is making your trading decisions for you.

Attention narrows and the need to act can overpower your usual rules. It may follow losses, missed opportunities, exhaustion or even excitement after wins. Recognizing the state matters more than inventing a better chart signal while you are in it.

**Hypothetical example.** Someone keeps reopening positions after being stopped out, no longer able to explain the trade beyond 'I need this to work'.

## Bounce
A rise after a fall. It can happen inside a longer downward trend without ending that trend.

A bounce can start because sellers pause, traders take profits on shorts or buyers see an opportunity. To judge whether it is becoming a reversal, compare later highs and lows rather than assuming any green candle ends the larger decline.

Hypothetical: 100 → 70 → 80 → 60. The move to 80 was a bounce, not a lasting recovery.

## Market bottom
A low point before a sustained recovery. Calling the bottom in real time is uncertain.

A bottom is defined relative to a period: the lowest point of a day can be followed by a lower price next week. A support area, a slowing decline or an indicator signal may offer clues, but none identifies a final low in advance.

A price looking cheap or a market feeling fearful does not establish that the lowest price has been reached.

## Bull market
A sustained period of generally rising prices. One strong day does not establish a bull market.

Rising prices can be interrupted by large pullbacks, and different assets can be in different phases at the same time. A bull market describes a broader tendency, not a guarantee that every asset or entry will make money.

Hypothetical: a recovery from 70 to 80 can still be part of a decline from 100. Look at the broader structure.

## Bear market
A sustained period of generally falling prices. A 20% fall from a recent high is a common convention, not a universal law.

The overall structure can remain weak despite powerful short-term rallies. The familiar percentage threshold is a shorthand, particularly for stock indexes; timeframe and market structure also matter. Crossing a threshold does not establish when the decline will end.

The label describes what has happened; it does not tell you how much further prices must fall.

## Correction
A decline after a rise. For stock indexes, a fall of about 10% from a recent high is commonly called a correction.

The label says that price has retreated from a recent high; it does not explain the cause. Profit-taking, changing expectations and forced selling can all contribute. Whether it remains a pullback or becomes a deeper decline is only resolved as events unfold.

A correction can end or deepen. Calling it a correction does not make the loss temporary or harmless.

## Bull trap
A rise that appears to signal a recovery but is followed by renewed falls.

Buyers may treat the first recovery or apparent breakout as confirmation, then find that demand does not continue. Selling and exits by disappointed buyers can add pressure. The trap is recognizable afterward; live, it can resemble the beginning of a genuine recovery.

Hypothetical: a price briefly moves above a watched level, attracts buyers and then falls back below it.

## Breakout
A move above a watched trading range or resistance area. It can fail.

Price moves beyond an area that previously contained it because trading now occurs at higher levels. Traders use different rules to distinguish a brief excursion from a sustained move. A breakout is therefore an observation plus a chosen confirmation method, not proof of future gains.

A close, extra trading activity or a retest may be used as confirmation rules; none guarantees that the move will last.

## Breakdown
A move below a watched support area or trading range. It can reverse.

A support area stops containing the decline and trades occur below it. That can trigger planned exits or attract new sellers, but buyers can also reclaim the area. Specify the timeframe and confirmation rule rather than calling every intraday dip a breakdown.

Hypothetical: support is around 100. A brief move to 99 followed by a recovery differs from several closes below it.

## Retest
When a price returns to an area it previously broke through, testing whether that area now attracts buying or selling.

The return may reveal whether participants still react around the old boundary. It can happen from above or below and may take several attempts. Not every breakout produces a retest, and not every retest leads to continuation.

Old support may become resistance, but it does not have to. Watch what actually happens rather than assuming a reaction.

## Moving average
An average of recent prices, updated as new periods arrive. It smooths the chart but reacts after prices change.

In a simple moving average, each new observation enters the calculation and the oldest drops out. Longer windows respond more slowly, while shorter ones follow recent changes more closely. This smoothing helps organize past movement rather than predict a price the market must reach.

Hypothetical: averaging the last 10 closing prices reduces day-to-day noise. A line based on more periods usually changes more slowly.

## 200-week moving average
An average based on 200 weeks of prices. Some traders watch it for long-term context; it is not a guaranteed floor.

For a simple version, add the closing prices of the latest 200 weeks and divide by 200. Each new week replaces the oldest one. Because it spans nearly four years, the line changes slowly and helps compare the current price with a long historical average.

**Hypothetical example.** If those weekly prices average $50 and the current price is $60, price is 20% above that historical average. That comparison describes where price sits; it does not prove the asset is expensive, cheap or about to reverse. Price can also move below the line and remain there.

## Seasonality
A tendency for returns or activity to differ by calendar period. A past monthly pattern may not repeat.

People look for recurring differences between months, weekdays or trading sessions. A pattern can reflect real behaviour, random variation or selective examples. Compare enough observations and consider whether market conditions changed before treating a calendar tendency as useful evidence.

A few strong Januaries are not a reliable forecast. Ask how many years were examined and whether weak examples were left out.

## Bitcoin halving
A scheduled reduction by half in the new-bitcoin portion of the reward paid for mining a block. It does not automatically halve Bitcoin's price or supply.

The scheduled change concerns the block subsidy, not transaction fees or coins already held by investors. Slower new issuance is a supply-side change, while market price still depends on how much people want to buy or sell and what they already anticipated.

A change in new issuance can matter, but demand, expectations and wider conditions also affect price. Few past cycles are a small sample.

## Market cycle
A broad sequence of changing market conditions, such as expansion, optimism, decline and recovery. Its timing is not fixed.

Cycle labels organize a messy history into phases such as accumulation, expansion and decline. The boundaries are subjective and phases can overlap or fail to resemble earlier examples. Similar human behaviour does not make the next cycle identical in timing or size.

A four-year comparison is an interpretation of limited history, not a clock forcing prices to follow a schedule.

## All-time high
The highest recorded price in a specified data series. Exchanges and data providers may report slightly different values.

The record depends on the instrument, currency, venue and whether you compare intraday highs or closing prices. A new record describes past trading, not a valuation judgment. Breaking it can attract attention without showing how much further the market can rise.

An asset that once reached a high is not guaranteed to return to it. The previous high is a reference, not a promise.

## Fear and Greed Index
A score summarizing selected measures of market sentiment. A low score is not an automatic buy or sell instruction.

The provider combines chosen inputs into a single reading, often between zero and 100. That simplifies a complicated mood into something easy to compare over time. It does not measure every investor's feelings or provide a probability that tomorrow will rise or fall.

Different providers use different ingredients. Extreme fear can last, and a sentiment score does not establish what prices do next.

## Momentum
The pace and strength of price changes. A decline can lose momentum while prices are still falling.

Direction asks whether prices are rising or falling; momentum asks about the strength or speed of that movement. A car slowing while moving backward has not yet started moving forward. Price-based momentum indicators help describe that distinction, but different calculations can disagree.

Hypothetical: 100 → 90 → 85 → 83. The losses get smaller, but the price has not started rising.

## RSI
A price-momentum indicator on a 0–100 scale, comparing recent gains and losses. It is not a count of buyers and sellers.

RSI compares average upward and downward changes over a selected number of periods, often 14. On a daily chart those are days; on an hourly chart they are hours. Changing the period or timeframe changes the information behind the score.

Below 30 is conventionally called oversold. That label does not mean cheap, safe or certain to rebound.

## Oversold
An indicator condition associated with strong recent downward movement. It does not mean the fall must end.

The threshold depends on the indicator and its settings, so first identify what is being measured. Oversold is about recent price behaviour, not the asset's fundamental value. It can coexist with bad news, deteriorating conditions and further losses.

A strongly falling market can stay oversold. The indicator describes recent movement rather than guaranteeing a reversal.

## Bullish divergence
When price makes a lower low but an indicator makes a higher low. It may suggest weakening downward momentum, not a confirmed recovery.

Compare matching swing lows on the price chart and the same indicator timeframe. The disagreement suggests the new low is not accompanied by the same indicator weakness. Divergences can persist through several lower lows, so they do not supply an exact entry time.

Hypothetical: price lows are 100 then 95, while RSI lows are 20 then 28. The disagreement is the divergence.

## MACD
An indicator comparing faster and slower moving averages to examine changes in momentum and trend.

Common settings compare a faster average of 12 periods with a slower one of 26; a signal line smooths the difference. The histogram usually shows the gap between MACD and that signal line. A crossover concerns those lines, not a guarantee that price has changed direction.

Negative histogram bars becoming smaller is not the same as prices rising. MACD and RSI both use price history, so they are not independent proof.

## Candlestick wick
The thin line showing prices reached beyond a candle's opening and closing prices during that period.

The top of the upper wick marks the highest traded price in that candle; the bottom of the lower wick marks the lowest. Wick length records an excursion beyond the body. Its meaning depends on the timeframe and surrounding price action, not on the wick alone.

Hypothetical: a price briefly falls below support and recovers before the candle closes. The wick records the visit, not a lasting break.

## Closing price
The last price recorded for a chosen candle or trading period. Crypto candles close even though the market trades around the clock.

Someone must define when the period ends. Different daily cut-off times can produce different candles from the same continuously traded market. A completed close fixes one period's result, while the next period can immediately move somewhere else.

A weekly candle still in progress can change colour. A close is one confirmation rule, not a guarantee.

## Double bottom
A possible reversal pattern with two lows near a similar area and a recovery between them. Two touches alone do not confirm a reversal.

The low points need not match to the cent. What matters is the broader shape and how price behaves between and after them. A second test can attract buying, but can also exhaust the remaining support before another decline.

Traders often watch for a move above the intervening high. The pattern can fail even after that move.

## Symmetrical triangle
A narrowing price range formed by lower highs and higher lows. The shape alone does not decide the breakout direction.

Draw one boundary through declining swing highs and another through rising swing lows. The shrinking space describes a narrowing range; drawing the lines involves judgment. Price can leave either side, and a short-lived move outside may later return inside.

A drawing extending years into the future is an extrapolation, not a timetable for the market.

## Wedge
A narrowing chart pattern whose two boundaries generally slope in the same direction. It differs from a symmetrical triangle.

In a rising wedge both boundaries rise while approaching each other; in a falling wedge both descend. The narrowing shape reflects the chosen swing points. Traders attach possible interpretations to it, but neither its name nor its slope guarantees the next direction.

Check the actual drawing. Lower highs combined with higher lows usually describe a symmetrical triangle instead.

## Long position
A position that benefits if the asset's price rises and loses if it falls, before costs.

A long's exposure can come from owning an asset or holding a contract linked to it. For the same number of units, each unit of price increase adds to the position's value. Leverage changes the impact on your own capital and can introduce forced-closure risk.

You can be long by owning the asset outright or through a derivative. The risks depend on how the position is built.

## Short position
A position that benefits if the asset's price falls and loses if it rises, before costs.

A short is commonly created by borrowing and selling an asset or using a derivative. Closing it generally requires buying back or offsetting the exposure. Rising prices can cause large losses, and an unhedged borrowed-asset short can have theoretically unlimited price risk.

Selling an asset you own is not automatically opening a short. A short creates exposure that benefits from further falls.

## Spot market
A market where you buy or sell the asset itself, rather than only a contract tracking its price.

A spot trade exchanges the asset at an agreed current price, subject to the venue's settlement and custody arrangements. Owning the asset does not remove price, security or platform risk. If borrowing is added, a spot-based position can still become leveraged.

Buying outright without borrowing does not create a leveraged liquidation price. It can still lose most or all of its value.

## Scalping
A trading style seeking small moves over very short periods, often seconds or minutes. Frequent trades also create costs.

Because the intended moves are small, spread, fees and execution quality can consume a large part of the result. A high number of trades does not itself create an advantage. Short holding periods also do not automatically make the exposure safer.

An advertised return is incomplete without the full record, losses, fees, capital and leverage used.

## Liquidation heatmap
A model estimating where leveraged positions might be forcibly closed if prices reach certain levels. It is not the order book.

The provider estimates concentrations using its available data and assumptions about positions and leverage. Bright bands represent potential forced activity if price gets there, not a precise inventory of orders already waiting. Existing positions can close and new ones can open before the level is reached.

Bright areas are estimates, not guaranteed destinations. News and ordinary spot orders can move prices even when nearby clusters are small.

## Liquidation cascade
A chain reaction in which forced position closures add orders that can trigger further forced closures.

When a forced exit pushes price further, other positions may reach their own margin thresholds. This feedback can accelerate a move in either direction. Its scale depends on positioning, collateral and available liquidity; not every rapid price change is caused by liquidations.

Falling prices can force longs to sell; rising prices can force shorts to buy. Both can amplify an existing move.

## Short squeeze
A sharp rise amplified by short sellers buying to close their positions, voluntarily or under pressure.

Short sellers may close to limit losses, meet margin demands or reduce exposure. Their closing purchases add demand to an already rising market, sometimes triggering more exits. Once that buying subsides, the price can stabilize or reverse rather than continue rising.

A squeeze need not mean the asset's long-term prospects improved. It describes part of the trading mechanism.

## V-shaped recovery
A sharp fall followed by a sharp rise. It describes a price path, not a promised outcome.

The letter is a description of the chart after a fast turnaround. It says little about who bought, why they bought or whether the move will last. Short-term rebounds can look V-shaped while the larger market trend remains weak.

A quiet or oversold market does not owe anyone a V-shaped recovery. Sideways movement or further falls are also possible.

## GDP
Gross domestic product: the value of goods and services produced within an economy over a period.

Reports may distinguish nominal output from real output adjusted for inflation. Growth can be measured quarter to quarter, year over year or at an annualized pace, so identical-looking percentages can mean different things. Initial estimates are also revised as more data arrive.

An annualized quarterly growth rate is not the same as growth during that quarter. Check the release date, unit and revisions.

## Interest rate
The cost of borrowing, or the return paid for lending or saving, expressed over a stated period.

To compare rates, check the time period, whether the rate is fixed or variable, and how interest compounds. The central bank's policy rate influences other rates but is not the rate every borrower receives. Inflation also affects the purchasing power of any interest earned.

Higher rates can affect borrowing costs and the appeal of safer interest-paying assets. Market reactions also depend on expectations.

## Inflation
A sustained rise in the overall price level. It reduces what a unit of money can buy.

A price index follows a basket of spending rather than every person's exact purchases. Lower inflation means prices are rising more slowly; it does not necessarily mean they are falling. Changes in the basket, comparison period and components can affect the headline reading.

One product becoming dearer does not establish general inflation. Different inflation measures track different baskets.

## Federal Reserve
The central bank of the United States. Its policy decisions influence financing conditions, but do not control each market price.

The Fed uses policy tools to influence credit conditions while pursuing its employment and price-stability objectives. Investors react to decisions and to changes in the expected future policy path. An unchanged rate can still move markets if the accompanying message surprises them.

Strong growth can change expectations about rate cuts. It does not by itself establish inflation, employment or Bitcoin's next move.

## FOMC
The Federal Reserve committee responsible for key US monetary-policy decisions, including the target range for its policy interest rate.

Markets examine the rate decision, statement and any accompanying projections or press conference. The important comparison is with expectations before the announcement, not simply whether a rate rises or falls. The same decision can be interpreted differently in different economic conditions.

A market bottom occurring near a past meeting does not prove the next meeting will create another bottom.

## IPO
An initial public offering: a company offers shares to public investors as part of becoming publicly traded.

An offering can include newly issued shares, shares sold by existing holders or both. The offer price and the later market price can differ substantially. Company quality, valuation, ownership restrictions and the amount actually available to trade all matter.

A well-known company can be a poor investment at an excessive price. Check the offering details rather than assuming an IPO is a bargain.

## Retail investor
An individual investing their own money, rather than investing on behalf of a large institution.

The label describes the investor's role, not their competence or a particular account balance. Individuals may face different costs, access and information from institutions, but institutions can also make bad decisions. Neither group's activity proves where prices will go.

Saying retail bought from insiders does not establish who held every side of every trade. That needs actual ownership and trading evidence.

## Conditional scenario
An if-then description of what could happen and what evidence would support or invalidate it. It is not a probability estimate.

State the condition, the possible outcome and what would contradict the idea. This helps separate observation from prediction and avoids defending a story whatever happens. A useful scenario remains revisable when new evidence arrives instead of being treated as a commitment.

Two scenarios do not automatically have 50% probability each, and may leave out sideways movement or failed breakouts.

## Gambler's fallacy
The mistaken belief that a streak alone makes the opposite result due. Markets are not independent coin flips, but a streak alone still proves little.

The error is treating the past sequence as a debt the next outcome must repay. Real markets can have dependence and changing conditions, so probabilities need evidence about those conditions. Merely saying a run is unusually long supplies no such evidence.

Eight red candles do not by themselves establish that the next candle is more likely to be green. Comparable evidence is needed.
