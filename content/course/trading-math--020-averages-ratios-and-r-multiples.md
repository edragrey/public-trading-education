Traders talk about "average win", "reward to risk 2:1" and "I made 3R". These sound technical, but they are only averages and ratios. This lesson makes them ordinary.

## Two words first

An **average** (the mean) is the total divided by how many things there are. Wins of $10, $30 and $20 add to $60, over 3 trades is an average win of **$20**.

A **ratio** compares two amounts. 2:1 ("two to one") means one is twice the other. Reward to risk of 2:1 means you aim to make twice what you risk.

## Why average results hide things

> **Hypothetical example.** Five trades: +$10, +$10, +$10, +$10, −$100. The average result is (40 − 100) ÷ 5 = **−$12 per trade**. Four wins out of five (80%) and still a loser. One big loss outweighed the small wins.

So an average is useful, but only alongside how big the biggest loss can be.

## R: your risk as a ruler

Different trades have different dollar sizes. To compare them fairly, traders measure results in **R**, where **1R is the amount you risked** on that trade.

- You risked $20 and made $40: you made **+2R**.
- You risked $20 and lost $20: you lost **−1R**.
- You risked $50 and made $25: you made **+0.5R**.

R turns every trade into the same unit, so a $20 trade and a $500 trade can sit in the same list.

> **Hypothetical example.** Ten trades in R: +2, −1, −1, +3, −1, +1, −1, −1, +2, −1. The wins add to +8, the losses to −6, total **+2R** over 10 trades, an average of **+0.2R per trade**.

That +0.2R is your expectancy, in R. It is the same idea as the expectancy lesson, in a cleaner unit.

## Ratios you will meet

- **Reward to risk** (R:R): how much you aim to make for each unit risked. 3:1 = aim for 3R for each 1R.
- **Win rate**: wins ÷ trades. 6 of 10 = 60%.
- **Profit factor**: total won ÷ total lost. $800 won and $400 lost = 2.0. Above 1 you are ahead.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting win rate without the average win and loss.
- Comparing trades by dollars instead of by R.
- Trusting an average from just a few trades.

<details><summary>Mean vs median</summary><p>The median is the middle value when sorted. If most trades are small and one is huge, the average is pulled up by the huge one but the median shows what is typical. Looking at both is more honest.</p></details>

## Check yourself

<details><summary>1. Results +$30, −$10, +$20. What is the average?</summary><p>Total $40 ÷ 3 trades = about +$13.33 per trade.</p></details>

<details><summary>2. You risk $40 and make $100. How many R?</summary><p>$100 ÷ $40 = +2.5R.</p></details>

<details><summary>3. You risk $25 and lose $50 because you moved your stop. What was that, in R?</summary><p>−2R. You lost twice what you planned to risk, which is why moving a stop is dangerous.</p></details>

<details><summary>4. Won $600 in total, lost $300 in total. Profit factor?</summary><p>600 ÷ 300 = 2.0.</p></details>

<details><summary>5. Does a 70% win rate guarantee profit?</summary><p>No. If your average loss is much bigger than your average win, you can still lose money.</p></details>

## Try it (no real money)

Write down your last five paper trades. For each, note what you risked and what you made, then convert them to R.

**Key idea:** R measures every trade against what you risked, so different trades can be compared fairly.
