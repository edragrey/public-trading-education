The last lesson covered rates and money. This one covers three more pieces of the macro picture, and shows how they come together as a market mood.

## Two words first

**Inflation** is how fast prices across the economy are rising. It reduces what each unit of money can buy.

**Risk-on** is a mood in which investors are happy to hold riskier assets such as stocks and crypto. **Risk-off** is the opposite: they move to safer places like cash, government bonds or the dollar.

## Inflation

- High inflation hurts savers and pushes central banks to **raise rates**, which can weigh on risky assets.
- Falling inflation can open the door to cuts, which helps the other way.
- Inflation reports are scheduled. The **surprise** versus expectations moves markets.

Some people hold coins as a hedge against inflation. This is an idea, not a rule: in many years crypto has behaved more like a risky growth asset than a safe store of value.

## CPI, concretely

The most watched inflation number in the US is the **CPI** (Consumer Price Index). It tracks the prices of a basket of things households buy, such as food, housing, fuel and clothes.

- It is published **monthly** by a US government agency, usually mid-month, at a fixed time.
- The headline shows the change over the last month and over the last year.
- **Core CPI** leaves out food and energy, which jump around, to show the underlying trend.
- Traders compare the result with the **forecast**. A higher-than-expected number can push rate expectations up and hit risky assets; a lower one can do the opposite.

> **Hypothetical example.** Forecasters expect annual CPI of 3.0%. The report says 3.4%. Markets quickly bet the Fed will keep rates higher for longer, and stocks and crypto drop within minutes. If it had said 3.0%, little might have happened.

Other releases matter in the same way, such as the monthly **jobs report**. All follow the pattern: the **surprise** moves the market, not the number alone.

## The dollar

Many assets are priced in US dollars. When the dollar **strengthens**, it can pressure assets priced in it, and when it weakens, it can help them. The link is common but not constant.

## Risk-on and risk-off

> **Hypothetical example.** A bad surprise hits global markets. Investors sell stocks and crypto and buy cash and government bonds. Crypto and stocks fall together even though the news had nothing to do with coins. That is **risk-off**. When calm returns, buyers come back: **risk-on**.

In recent years, big crypto coins have often moved **with** stock markets during stress, especially tech stocks. That link **changes over time**. Sometimes crypto moves on its own news.

## Why this matters to you

- A crypto trade can lose because of a **non-crypto event**.
- When many assets fall together, spreading across coins gives less protection than you think (the diversification lesson).
- A calendar of scheduled events helps you avoid being surprised.

## How to use this sensibly

- Know the dates of major data releases and central bank meetings.
- Reduce size or step aside around big events if you cannot judge them.
- Do not build a position on a macro prediction. Use macro as context for risk, not as a reason to bet.

## Common mistakes

- Treating inflation as the only driver.
- Assuming crypto is always a safe haven or always tied to stocks.
- Trading a data release without a plan.
