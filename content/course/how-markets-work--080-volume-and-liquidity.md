A price on a chart looks the same whether millions of dollars changed hands or almost nothing did. This lesson shows how to tell the difference, and why it matters a lot for your risk.

## Two words first

**Volume** is how much was traded in a period: how many coins or shares changed hands, or their total value. Most charts show it as bars along the bottom.

**Liquidity** is how easily you can buy or sell without moving the price much. Lots of orders waiting in the book, close to the current price, means high liquidity.

## Why they matter

> **Hypothetical example.** Two made-up coins, both priced at $1.

| | Coin A | Coin B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traded in the last 24 hours | $500 million | $40,000 |
| Spread | 0.01% | 3% |
| Selling $2,000 straight away | Barely moves the price | Might push it down 5% or more |

Both charts show "$1". But in coin B, just getting in and out could cost you several percent, before the price has moved at all. And your stop might fill far below where you set it, because there's nobody waiting to buy.

## What thin markets do to your plan

Thin markets cause three problems (Survive first looks at the costs in detail):

- **Spread** is wider, so every trade starts with a bigger loss.
- **Slippage** is bigger, so stops fill at worse prices.
- **Sudden moves** are easier, because a single large order can push the price a long way.

All of this makes your planned loss less reliable. A 1% risk on paper can become 2% or 3% in reality.

## Volume and price moves

Volume also adds context to a move:

- A price move on **high volume** means lots of people took part.
- A move on **very low volume** means only a few trades made it happen. It may say little about what most participants think.

This describes what happened. It doesn't tell you what happens next.

## Be careful with reported volume

Some smaller exchanges and tokens have been found to inflate their volume with fake trades (sometimes called wash trading). If a tiny token shows huge volume on one little-known exchange only, be sceptical. Volume spread across several well-known venues is more believable.

## Two quick checks before any trade

1. **What's the spread?** Look at the order book. If it's more than a fraction of a percent, think twice.
2. **Could you get out?** Look at how much is waiting on the bid side near the price. If your position is a big chunk of it, you'll struggle to sell without moving the price.

## Try it (no real money)

Pick two coins or stocks: one very well known, one small. For each, find the 24-hour volume and the spread in the order book. Write them side by side.

**Key idea:** A price is only as reliable as the trading behind it. Check the volume and the spread before you trust a chart.
