Fourth case study, based on the US Securities and Exchange Commission's staff report on the event.

## What happened

In January 2021 the share price of **GameStop (GME)** and several other stocks rose dramatically. The SEC staff report says bullish sentiment among individual investors on social media was a major factor, and that GME combined five things: large price moves, large volume changes, large **short interest** (many traders betting on a fall), frequent Reddit mentions, and heavy mainstream media coverage.

Some numbers from the report:

- On **22 January 2021**, GME rose from $43 to $72, a **71%** increase in about three hours.
- Volume reached about **100 million shares a day**, an increase of **over 1,400%** from earlier levels.
- In the last week of January, daily moves ranged from a **rise of about $200** between January 26 and 27 to a **fall of about $154** between January 27 and 28.
- Bid-ask spreads **widened significantly**.
- As the end of January approached, several retail brokers **temporarily prohibited certain activity** (for example opening new positions) in these stocks and options.

## The mechanics, in plain words

1. Many traders had **shorted** the stock (see the long and short lesson).
2. Buying pushed the price up. Shorts who needed to close had to **buy back**, which pushed it up further. That's a **short squeeze**.
3. Prices moved so fast that brokers faced higher **margin and capital requirements** and some limited trading.

## What it teaches a trader

- **Crowd-driven moves can be extreme and sudden.** In either direction.
- **Shorts have no ceiling.** (See the shorting lesson.)
- **Spreads widen and slippage rises** in a frenzy. Your fill can be far from the price you saw.
- **Brokers can restrict you.** You may not be able to trade when you most want to.
- **A move already up 1,000% is not "cheap" or "expensive". It's unknowable.** Chasing late is the FOMO lesson at full volume.
- **Position size is the only protection.** Stops can gap.

## Both sides lost money

Some late buyers lost heavily after the peak. Some shorts lost heavily on the way up. Others gained. The point isn't who "won", it's how little control any participant had.

## Try it (no real money)

Imagine you're short 100 shares at $20 with a stop at $25, and the price opens at $60. Work out your loss vs the loss you planned. Write down what position size would have kept it tolerable.

**Key idea:** Crowds can move prices far beyond anything you plan for. Keep size small, avoid chasing, and never short without a limit on the damage.
