Here's something that confuses almost every beginner: two people look at the same coin at the same moment. One says it's going up. The other says it's going down. They can both be right, because they're looking at different **timeframes**.

## One word first

**Timeframe** is how much time each candle on a chart covers. On a 1-hour chart, each candle is one hour. On a daily chart, each candle is one day.

Common ones: 1 minute, 5 minutes, 15 minutes, 1 hour, 4 hours, 1 day, 1 week.

## Same coin, three stories

> **Hypothetical example.** One made-up coin, looked at three ways on the same afternoon.

| Timeframe | What you see |
| --- | --- |
| Weekly | Down 30% over the last three months. A long fall. |
| Daily | Up 12% over the last week. A bounce. |
| 15-minute | Down 2% in the last hour. A small dip. |

None of these is "the truth". Each one answers a different question: what's happened over months, over days, over the last hour.

## Shorter timeframes are noisier

The shorter the timeframe, the more random wobble you see. On a 1-minute chart, a lot of the movement is just individual orders bumping the price around. On a daily or weekly chart, those wobbles average out and bigger moves stand out.

Very short timeframes also mean more trades, more fees and more decisions, usually made faster and with more emotion. For beginners, that's a lot of cost for not much learning.

## Matching the timeframe to the trade

The timeframe you use should match how long you plan to hold.

- Planning to hold for **weeks**: look mostly at daily and weekly charts.
- Planning to hold for **days**: daily and 4-hour charts.
- Planning to hold for **hours**: 1-hour and 15-minute charts.

A common habit is to **check one timeframe higher** than the one you trade on. If you trade on the daily, glance at the weekly first. It tells you what bigger picture your trade sits inside.

## Timeframes and your stop

This connects to the next module, Survive first. A stop placed using a 5-minute chart will usually be close to your entry (small wobbles). A stop placed using a daily chart will usually be further away (bigger swings). Neither is wrong, but your **position size must follow** the stop distance, as the position size lesson in Survive first will show.

## Common mistakes

- Looking at one timeframe and ignoring the one above it.
- Setting a stop that suits a 5-minute chart on a trade meant to last days.
- Switching timeframes until the chart says what you want.

## Try it (no real money)

Pick one market and look at it on the weekly, daily and 1-hour charts. Write one sentence for each: "Over this period, it has ___." Notice how different the three sentences are.

**Key idea:** A chart's story depends on its timeframe. Choose the one that matches how long you plan to hold, and check one level higher.
