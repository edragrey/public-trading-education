If you trade stocks, you're trading slices of real businesses. You don't need an accounting degree. A few numbers go a long way.

## Three reports, three questions

Public companies publish regular reports with three main statements:

- **Income statement:** did the company make money over a period?
- **Balance sheet:** what does it own and owe right now?
- **Cash flow statement:** how much real cash came in and went out?

## The words to know

- **Revenue (sales):** all money coming in from customers.
- **Profit (earnings or net income):** what's left after all costs.
- **EPS (earnings per share):** profit divided by the number of shares.
- **Margin:** profit as a share of revenue. A 10% margin means 10 cents of profit on each dollar of sales.
- **Debt:** money owed. A lot of debt makes a company more fragile when times are hard.
- **Guidance:** what the company says it expects next.

> **Hypothetical example.** A company has revenue of $1,000 million and net profit of $100 million. Margin = 100 ÷ 1,000 = **10%**. With 50 million shares, EPS = $100m ÷ 50m = **$2 per share**.

## Valuation: price compared to what you get

The **P/E ratio** is the share price divided by EPS. If the price is $40 and EPS is $2, P/E = **20**. You're paying $20 for each $1 of yearly profit.

A high P/E isn't automatically bad. It can mean the market expects fast growth. A low P/E isn't automatically cheap. It may mean the market expects trouble. P/E is useful for **comparing similar companies**, not for deciding alone.

## Earnings season

Companies report results every quarter. Prices often move sharply on the day. The move depends less on whether results were good and more on whether they beat **expectations**.

> **Hypothetical example.** A company reports profit up 15%. Analysts expected 25%. The price may fall even though results were good.

Earnings days are risky: prices can jump past your stop (a gap). Many traders avoid or shrink positions around them.

## A good company isn't always a good trade

A great business can already be priced as a great business. You can lose money on a good company if you pay too much or the market falls. The reverse also happens.

## Common mistakes

- Buying because you like the brand.
- Looking at one number, like P/E, alone.
- Holding through earnings without realising the risk.
- Treating company guidance as a promise.

## Check yourself

<details><summary>1. Revenue $400m, profit $20m. Margin?</summary><p>20 ÷ 400 = 5%.</p></details>

<details><summary>2. Price $60, EPS $3. P/E?</summary><p>20.</p></details>

<details><summary>3. Why can a stock fall after good results?</summary><p>Results may have been worse than expectations.</p></details>

<details><summary>4. What does a lot of debt do to a company's risk?</summary><p>It makes it more fragile when business slows.</p></details>

<details><summary>5. Is a good company always a good trade?</summary><p>No. The price you pay and the market still matter.</p></details>

## Try it (no real money)

Pick one listed company you know. Find its latest quarterly report. Write down revenue, profit, margin and EPS, and whether they rose or fell compared with the same quarter last year.

**Key idea:** A share is a slice of a business. A few numbers (revenue, profit, margin, debt) describe it, but price versus expectations is what moves the stock.
