Last lesson said a system needs to be written down. This lesson gives you the page to write it on. Keep it to **one page**: if it doesn't fit, it's too complicated to follow under pressure.

## Two words first

**Trade plan** is your system on one page: the rules that apply to every trade.

**Setup** is the specific situation you're looking for before you'll consider a trade. For example "price pulls back to a support area in an uptrend on the daily chart".

## The template

Copy this somewhere you'll see it. Fill in every line.

```
MY TRADE PLAN
Version: ___   Date: ________

1. Markets I trade:  ____________
2. Timeframe: ______
   I also check: ______
3. My setup, in one sentence:
   ___________________________
4. I get in when: ____________
5. My stop goes: _____________
6. My target goes: ___________
7. Risk per trade: ___%
8. Max trades a day: ___
9. Stop for the day after
   losing: ___%
10. I don't trade when:
   ___________________________
```
## A filled-in example

> **Hypothetical example.** One possible beginner plan, to show the level of detail. It's not a recommendation, and the setup isn't claimed to be profitable.

1. **Markets:** two large, liquid coins only.
2. **Timeframe:** daily. I also check the weekly.
3. **Setup:** in a weekly uptrend, the price pulls back to an area where it turned up at least twice before.
4. **Get in:** when a daily candle closes back above that area.
5. **Stop:** just below the low of the pullback.
6. **Target:** near the last high before the pullback. If that's less than 2:1, I skip the trade.
7. **Risk:** 1% of my account.
8. **Max trades per day:** 1.
9. **Stop for the day after losing:** 2%.
10. **Don't trade:** in the 24 hours before a major interest rate decision, or when I'm tired, upset or chasing a loss.

Notice every line links back to an earlier lesson: timeframes, support, stops, risk and reward, risk per trade, big events.

## Rules for the rules

- **Specific beats vague.** "When it looks good" isn't a rule. "When a daily candle closes back above the area" is.
- **Fewer markets is easier.** One or two well-known, liquid markets give you less to watch and more comparable trades.
- **Date every version.** You'll change it (the lesson on changing your rules explains when). Dating it lets you compare.
- **Plan the "no".** Line 10 is as important as line 4. Most good systems spend most of their time saying no.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a plan so long it's never read.
- Leaving the stop out of the template.
- Not testing whether you could follow it on a bad day.

## Check yourself

<details><summary>1. Is "I buy when the chart looks bullish" a usable rule?</summary><p>No. It's too vague. Someone else couldn't follow it and get the same trade. Describe exactly what you see.</p></details>

<details><summary>2. Why keep the plan to one page?</summary><p>So you can actually check it before every trade, including when you're stressed.</p></details>

<details><summary>3. Why date each version of your plan?</summary><p>So you know which trades were taken under which rules, and can compare versions fairly.</p></details>

<details><summary>4. Why keep the plan to one page?</summary><p>A short plan can be followed under pressure.</p></details>

<details><summary>5. What makes a rule 'followable'?</summary><p>It can be answered yes or no without interpretation.</p></details>

## Try it (no real money)

Fill in the template with your own answers, using Trade 1 from How markets work as the example. Where you don't know yet, write your best guess and mark it with a question mark. Then read it as if it belonged to a stranger: could they follow it?

**Key idea:** Put your whole system on one page. If a rule can't be written plainly, it can't be followed under pressure.
