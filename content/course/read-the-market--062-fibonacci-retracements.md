After a strong move up, price often drops back a bit before continuing. That drop is a **pullback** (also called a retracement). Traders want to know where it might stop. **Fibonacci retracements** are one popular answer.

## Two words first

- **Swing high / swing low:** the top and bottom of a clear move on the chart.
- **Retracement:** the part of that move that price gives back.

## How it works

You pick the start and end of a clear move and the chart draws horizontal lines at set fractions of it. The usual ones are **38.2%, 50% and 61.8%**. (The 50% isn't truly a Fibonacci number; people add it anyway.)

> **Hypothetical example.** A coin rises from $100 to $200, a $100 move. A pullback to 50% is $150. A pullback to 38.2% is $161.80. A pullback to 61.8% is $138.20. A trader watches those three areas to see whether buyers show up.

The calculation is just this: **high − (percent × size of the move)**. For 61.8% here: $200 − (0.618 × $100) = $138.20.

## Why people think it works

Honestly, partly because so many traders watch the same lines. If lots of people place buy orders near the same areas, price can pause there. That's a **self-fulfilling** effect, not a law of nature. There's no proof that these exact numbers are special.

## How to use it sensibly

- Use the levels as **areas to watch**, not exact prices. A pullback that pauses near 50% to 61.8% is fine; one that stops at precisely 61.8% is luck.
- Combine them with things you can see: an old support or resistance, a moving average, a trendline. When several land in the same area, it matters more.
- Wait for a sign that buyers are returning (a strong candle, volume) before acting, and always have a stop.
- If price falls through the 61.8% area and keeps going, the idea has failed. Accept it.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the lines from random points until one fits afterwards.
- Treating a level as a guarantee and skipping the stop.
- Buying a level in a downtrend because it "should" bounce.

## Go deeper

<details><summary>Who is Fibonacci?</summary><p>Leonardo Fibonacci was a medieval mathematician who described a number sequence (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...) where each number is the sum of the two before. Ratios between the numbers in that sequence give 0.618 and 0.382. Traders borrowed them. Nature does show the sequence sometimes; markets are made of people, not plants.</p></details>

<details><summary>Using it to set targets</summary><p>Some traders also draw extensions above the old high to guess profit targets. The same caution applies: these are areas to plan around, not promises.</p></details>

## Check yourself

<details><summary>1. What is a retracement?</summary><p>The part of an earlier move that price gives back before continuing or reversing.</p></details>

<details><summary>2. A coin rises from $50 to $100. Where is the 50% retracement?</summary><p>$100 − (0.5 × $50) = $75.</p></details>

<details><summary>3. Why might price pause at these levels?</summary><p>Many traders watch them, so orders cluster there. It's partly self-fulfilling.</p></details>

<details><summary>4. Is a bounce at exactly 61.8% proof the method is exact?</summary><p>No. Price bounces near areas, and you can find a level that fits in hindsight almost anywhere.</p></details>

<details><summary>5. What makes a level more meaningful?</summary><p>Other things pointing at the same area, such as old support, a moving average or a trendline.</p></details>

## Try it (no real money)

Pick one clear move on a chart. Draw the retracement levels. Note where the pullback actually stopped, and whether it was near an old support or resistance too. Repeat on two more charts and write down how often it paused near a level.

**Key idea:** Fibonacci retracements mark areas where a pullback might pause. They work as a planning tool, especially next to other levels, and never as a guarantee.
