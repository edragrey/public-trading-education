Macroeconomics looks at the whole economy at once. The big idea for traders: **the price and amount of money change what people do with risky assets.**

## Two words first

A **central bank** (such as the US Federal Reserve) is the public body that manages a country's money and sets its main interest rate.

The **policy rate** is that main interest rate. It influences what banks charge each other and, through that, what loans cost for everyone.

## How rates reach markets

- **Higher rates:** borrowing is more expensive, and savings pay more. Safe options like bank deposits and government bonds look better, so riskier assets can be less attractive.
- **Lower rates:** borrowing is cheaper and savings pay little. People look for returns elsewhere, which can support stocks and crypto.

It is not a rule, but the **direction of travel** often matters more than the level. Markets react to what they expect rates to do next, not only to what they are now.

> **Hypothetical example.** Rates are 5%, and the central bank hints it may cut them. Even before any cut, prices of risky assets may rise as people expect cheaper money. If the cut was already expected, the announcement can be a non-event.

## The money supply and "liquidity"

Central banks can also change how much money is in the system:

- **Creating money to buy bonds** (often called quantitative easing) adds money to the system.
- **Letting bonds mature without replacing them** (quantitative tightening) removes it.

When people say "global liquidity is rising", they usually mean there is more money around looking for a home. Many traders think this helps risky assets, but it is a **tendency, not a guarantee**.

## The US Federal Reserve, concretely

The US central bank is the **Federal Reserve** (the Fed). Its rate-setting committee, the **FOMC**, meets about eight times a year. Each meeting ends with three things markets read closely:

1. **The rate decision:** raise, cut or hold the policy rate.
2. **The statement:** a short text. Small word changes ("patient", "data dependent") get studied for clues.
3. **The press conference:** the chair answers questions, and tone can move markets in minutes.

Four times a year the Fed also publishes projections, including the **dot plot**: where each member expects rates to be later. Traders compare it with what markets already expected.

The Fed's two jobs are low unemployment and stable prices, and it aims for about **2% inflation** over time. That is why inflation data (next lesson) and jobs data matter so much: they shape what the Fed does next.

> **Hypothetical example.** Before a meeting, markets expect a hold. The Fed holds, but the chair sounds more worried about inflation than expected, hinting at future hikes. Even with no rate change, stocks and crypto may fall, because the **message** was tighter than expected.

Rate moves in the US matter globally because the dollar sits at the centre of world finance. Other central banks (the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan, the People's Bank of China) matter too, and differences between them move currencies and capital.

## Why traders watch central banks

- Meetings and statements can move many markets at once.
- Surprises matter more than the news itself.
- Words and tone can move prices as much as numbers.

## What not to do

- Do not trade the exact minute of a big announcement without a plan: spreads widen and moves are wild.
- Do not assume a rate cut always means prices go up.
- Do not build one big bet on a macro story.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking "rates up means crypto down" always holds.
- Ignoring expectations: it is the surprise that moves things.
- Mixing up the interest rate with the amount of money in the system.
