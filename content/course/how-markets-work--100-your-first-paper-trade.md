You now know what a market is, where you trade, what a price is, the order types, long and short, and how to read a candle. Time to use them, with **no real money**.

A **paper trade** is a practice trade that uses pretend money. Many brokers and exchanges offer a demo or paper account. If yours doesn't, a notebook or spreadsheet works.

## Before you click anything: the plan

Write these five lines first. This is the habit that matters most.

```
Market / asset:
Why I'm taking it (one sentence):
Entry (order type + price):
Exit if I'm wrong (stop price):
Exit if I'm right (target, or "I'll reassess"):
```

The Survive first module shows how to size a trade from your stop and why about 1% risk is a sensible limit. For now, use a **small, fixed** pretend risk.

## A worked example (hypothetical)

> **Hypothetical example.** Pretend account: $10,000. Pretend risk per trade: $100 (1%).

| Line | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Asset | A coin trading near $50 |
| Why | The price has held above $48 several times and is bouncing |
| Entry | Buy limit at $50 |
| Exit if wrong | Stop-loss at $48 (that's $2 below entry) |
| Exit if right | Sell limit at $54 (a gain of $4 per coin) |

**Size:** the stop is $2 away. We accept a $100 loss, so $100 ÷ $2 = **50 coins**.

- If the stop hits: 50 × $2 = **-$100**.
- If the target hits: 50 × $4 = **+$200**.

That's a 2-to-1 reward for the risk. It still might lose. Many sensible trades do.

## Step by step

1. **Pick one asset** you can follow. Look at the chart on two timeframes.
2. **Fill in the plan** above before you place anything.
3. **Work out the size** from the stop distance and your pretend risk.
4. **Place the entry** with the order type you chose. Notice if it fills or not.
5. **Place the stop right away.** An unprotected position isn't a plan.
6. **Leave it alone.** Don't move the stop further away because you hope.
7. **Close it** at the stop, the target or your written reason.
8. **Review it** in two lines: what happened, and did I follow my plan?

## What the review is for

The result matters less than the review. A trade that loses while following your plan is a good trade. A trade that wins after you broke your own rules is a warning.

## Common mistakes

- Skipping the plan "because it's only pretend". If you practise carelessly, you'll trade carelessly.
- Not placing a stop on a paper trade.
- Treating a few wins in a row as proof.
- Paper trading with huge pretend sizes you'd never use.

## Check yourself

<details><summary>1. What are the five lines of the trade plan?</summary><p>Asset, reason, entry, exit if wrong, exit if right.</p></details>

<details><summary>2. Pretend risk is $50. Entry $20, stop $19. How many units?</summary><p>$50 ÷ $1 = 50 units.</p></details>

<details><summary>3. When should you place the stop?</summary><p>Right after the entry fills, before doing anything else.</p></details>

<details><summary>4. A paper trade loses but you followed your plan. Good or bad trade?</summary><p>A good trade. The process was right even though this one lost.</p></details>

<details><summary>5. Why start with small real size later?</summary><p>Real money brings emotions that practice doesn't, so you learn how you behave with little at stake.</p></details>

## Try it (no real money)

Do exactly one paper trade using the steps above and write the two-line review. Come back to it after a week and ask yourself whether you'd make the same plan again.

**Key idea:** Plan first, size from your stop, protect the trade, then review. Practise the process before the money is real.
