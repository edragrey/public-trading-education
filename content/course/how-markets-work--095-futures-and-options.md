So far you've met buying the real thing (spot). Many markets also trade **derivatives**: contracts whose value comes from the price of something else.

## Two words first

- **Derivative:** a contract based on the price of another asset, such as oil, a stock or a coin.
- **Contract:** an agreement with set terms. You trade the agreement, not the asset.

## Futures

A **future** is an agreement to buy or sell something at a set price on a set date. You don't need to take delivery; most traders close the contract before it ends and keep the profit or loss.

> **Hypothetical example.** An oil future says "buy 1,000 barrels at $80 on the last day of next month". If oil rises to $90, the contract has gained $10 per barrel, or $10,000. If oil falls to $70, it has lost the same. Both sides are **obliged** to settle.

Why use them?
- **Hedging:** a farmer can lock in a price for the crop; an airline can lock in a fuel price.
- **Speculation:** traders bet on price going up or down, often with leverage.

In crypto, the common version is the **perpetual future**, which has no end date. You met it in the Survive first module, with its funding payments and liquidation risk.

## Options

An **option** gives you the **right, but not the obligation**, to buy or sell at a set price before a set date.

- A **call** is the right to buy.
- A **put** is the right to sell.
- You pay a fee called the **premium** to get that right.

> **Hypothetical example.** You pay $2 for a call giving you the right to buy a stock at $50 until next month. If the stock goes to $60, your right is worth about $10, a big gain on $2. If it stays at $50, your right expires worthless and you lose the $2. The most you can lose when you **buy** an option is the premium.

Selling options is a different story: the seller takes the premium but can face very large losses.

## Why options are not for beginners

- They depend on price, time **and** volatility, so they are hard to value.
- Most options expire worthless, because the move you need often doesn't happen in time.
- Selling them can lose far more than you put in.

You don't need options to be a good trader. Learn spot and simple trades well first.

## Common mistakes

- Buying cheap, short-dated options as "lottery tickets" and calling it a plan.
- Selling options for the premium without understanding the loss risk.
- Treating a future like owning the asset. It can be liquidated, and it can end.

## Try it (no real money)

Look up an option chain for a well-known stock on a free site. Find one call and one put. Write down the premium, the set price and the expiry date, and the move needed for the call buyer to break even.

**Key idea:** Futures are obligations tied to a price; options are rights you pay for. Both are tools for experienced traders, and neither is needed to start.
