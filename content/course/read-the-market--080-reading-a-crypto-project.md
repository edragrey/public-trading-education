Thousands of coins exist, and many are copies, jokes or scams. You can't know everything, but a short checklist filters out a lot of trouble. This isn't about finding winners. It's about avoiding obvious losers.

## The six questions

1. **What does it do, in one sentence?** If you can't say it without buzzwords, you don't understand it yet. Examples: "a payment network", "a way to lend and borrow without a bank", "a joke coin with no purpose".
2. **Who is behind it?** Is the team public and traceable? Anonymous isn't always bad, but you then rely entirely on the code and the incentives.
3. **Who holds the supply?** Look at the holder list on a blockchain explorer. If a few wallets hold most coins, they can move the price by selling. Check the token unlock schedule too (see the market cap lesson).
4. **Does anyone use it?** Look for real activity: users, fees paid, money locked. Be careful: numbers can be faked or boosted by rewards that later stop.
5. **How does it make money, and where does value go?** Some projects earn fees. Others only exist if new buyers keep arriving.
6. **Is the code checked?** Has an independent firm audited it? An audit lowers risk; it never removes it.

## Red flags

- Promises of fixed or guaranteed returns.
- Pressure to buy fast, or rewards for bringing friends.
- A website with vague text and a lot of hype.
- Liquidity that can be withdrawn by the creator (so-called "rug pulls").
- Team or influencers who can sell at any time, with no disclosure.
- You can buy but struggle to sell.

> **Hypothetical example.** A new token claims "100x guaranteed". The top three wallets hold 70% of the supply, the team is anonymous, and the only way to make money is if more people buy. That fails questions 2, 3 and 5. You don't need to predict anything. You can just pass.

## What the checklist can't do

It won't tell you the price will rise. Good projects fall in bad markets, and weak ones sometimes rise on hype. A checklist reduces the chance of obvious traps and helps you size small when risk is high.

## Common mistakes

- Trusting a project because a friend or influencer likes it.
- Judging by the price chart alone.
- Confusing a large following with a large use.
- Ignoring the holder list.
