Before charts and orders, one simple question: what is a market, and what are people actually trading?

## A market is a meeting place

A **market** is anywhere buyers and sellers can find each other and agree on a price. A street market with fruit stalls is one. So is a stock exchange, only faster and with more zeros.

Two things make any market work:

- **Something to trade.** A share, a coin, a barrel of oil, a currency.
- **A way to match people.** Usually an exchange or a broker that keeps a list of who wants to buy and sell (you'll meet that list in the next lessons).

## The main kinds of market

| Market | What you trade | Example | Usually open |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Stocks** | Small slices of ownership in a company | A share of a company | Weekdays, set hours |
| **Crypto** | Digital coins and tokens | Bitcoin, Ether | 24 hours, every day |
| **Commodities** | Raw materials | Oil, gold, wheat | Mostly weekdays, long hours |
| **Currencies (forex)** | One money against another | Euro against US dollar | Weekdays, nearly around the clock |
| **Bonds** | Loans to a government or company | A government bond | Weekdays, set hours |

You don't need to trade all of them. Most beginners are better off learning **one** well.

## Owning vs betting on price

There are two very different ways to take part:

- **Owning the thing.** You buy a share or a coin and hold it. If it goes up, you gain. This is called trading the **spot** market.
- **Trading a contract about its price.** You never own the share or coin. You hold a contract that gains or loses as the price moves. These are called **derivatives**: futures, options and perpetual contracts are examples. They often allow **leverage**, which makes gains and losses bigger. The leverage lessons in Survive first cover why that's risky.

> **Hypothetical example.** You buy 1 coin at $100. That's spot: you hold the coin. Alternatively you open a contract that tracks the coin's price. You hold no coin, only a bet that moves with it. Same chart, very different risks.

## Why traders prefer some markets

- **Liquid** markets have lots of buyers and sellers, so you can get in and out easily at a fair price.
- **Volatile** markets move a lot, which means bigger chances and bigger risks.
- **Hours** matter: a market that's closed overnight can jump between the close and the next open. A 24-hour market can move while you sleep.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking every market works the same. Hours, costs and risks differ.
- Jumping into derivatives before understanding spot.
- Choosing a market because it's exciting rather than because you can learn it.

## Check yourself

<details><summary>1. What two things does a market need to work?</summary><p>Something to trade, and a way to match buyers and sellers.</p></details>

<details><summary>2. What's the difference between spot and derivatives?</summary><p>Spot means you own the actual share or coin. A derivative is a contract that follows the price without you owning the thing.</p></details>

<details><summary>3. Which of these markets is open 24 hours a day, every day: stocks, crypto or bonds?</summary><p>Crypto.</p></details>

<details><summary>4. Why can a market that closes overnight "gap" at the open?</summary><p>News and orders build up while it's closed, so the first price of the day can be far from the last one.</p></details>

<details><summary>5. Why might a beginner pick one market and stick to it?</summary><p>Each market has its own hours, costs and habits. Learning one well beats knowing five badly.</p></details>

## Try it (no real money)

Pick one market that interests you. Write down: what you'd trade, what hours it's open, and whether you'd trade spot or a contract. Keep it in a notes file; you'll use it in the first paper trade lesson.

**Key idea:** A market is a meeting place for buyers and sellers. Spot means owning the thing, derivatives mean a contract about its price, and the second is riskier.
