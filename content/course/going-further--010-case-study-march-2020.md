This is the first of four short case studies. Each takes a real event, sticks to what's documented, and asks what a beginner can learn. They look at the past; they predict nothing.

## What happened

In early 2020 the COVID-19 pandemic caused sweeping disruption and "unprecedented uncertainty", as a Treasury advisory committee later described it. Business and government lockdowns arrived in March. Volatility jumped, credit spreads widened, and share prices fell fast.

Then something less expected happened. Official reports describe a **"dash for cash"**: a broad range of investors tried to raise cash at the same time, and sold even very safe assets to do it. A US Treasury staff report says that in March 2020 the market for US government bonds, normally the deepest and safest in the world, was **significantly disrupted**, and that market liquidity deteriorated as selling pressure rose.

On **March 18, 2020** the Federal Reserve, with the approval of the Treasury Secretary, stepped in with emergency support, according to a report of the President's Working Group on Financial Markets.

## Why this matters to a trader

1. **Correlations jump in a crisis.** Things that usually move differently fell together, because everyone wanted cash. Diversification helped less than people expected (see the diversification lesson).
2. **Liquidity can vanish.** Even a huge, safe market became hard to trade for a while. In a thinner market, spreads widen and slippage grows (see the order types and volume lessons).
3. **"Safe" is relative.** Cash and short-term government bills were what people reached for. Almost everything else was sold for them.
4. **Fast moves cut through stops.** In extreme days, prices can gap past your stop.
5. **Scheduled and unscheduled events both matter.** Nobody had this one on a calendar.

## What was done well, and badly

Traders with small size and a loss limit lived to trade again. Those who were leveraged, or who held positions they couldn't explain, were the ones forced to sell at the worst time.

## Questions to ask yourself

- If everything I hold fell together next week, how much would I lose?
- What would I do if I couldn't exit at my stop?
- Do I know what I own, and why?

## What this case can't tell you

It doesn't say crashes can be predicted, or that every crash recovers the way this one did. Later events differ. It simply shows how much correlation and liquidity matter when stress arrives.

## Try it (no real money)

Write a one-paragraph "stress plan": what you would do if all your positions fell together in one day, and how much you could lose.

**Key idea:** In a real crisis, correlations jump and liquidity dries up. Small size and no leverage are what keep you in the game.
