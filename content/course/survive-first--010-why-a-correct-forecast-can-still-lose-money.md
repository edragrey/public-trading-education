Most beginners think the hard part of trading is guessing where a price will go. This lesson shows why that is only half the story, and why this module starts with surviving.

## A simple example

> **Hypothetical example.** This stock is made up, but the arithmetic is exact.

1. You buy a stock at **$100**, because you think it's going to **$150**.
2. First, it falls. All the way to **$65**.
3. You get nervous, and you sell. That's a **35% loss** ($100 to $65).
4. Then the stock recovers and climbs to exactly **$150**.

| | Your forecast | Your trade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happened | The price reached $150 | You sold at $65 |
| Result | Right | **−35%** |

Your forecast was right. Your trade lost money.

## Why getting back is harder than it looks

Losses and gains are not symmetrical. If you lose 35%, you have $65 left. To get back to $100, those $65 must grow by $35, which is about **54%**.

The rule is: **gain needed = loss ÷ (1 − loss)**. For a 35% loss, that's 0.35 ÷ 0.65 ≈ 0.54.

| If you lose | You need to gain |
| --- | --- |
| 10% | about 11% |
| 25% | about 33% |
| 35% | about 54% |
| 50% | 100% |

The bigger the loss, the faster the climb back gets steep. That's why careful traders care so much about keeping losses small.

## This isn't only theory

In 2020, the S&P 500 (an index of 500 large US companies) fell from 3,386.15 on 19 February to 2,237.40 on 23 March. That's about **−34% in five weeks**.

By 18 August 2020 it closed at a new record high (3,389.78). Someone who sold in March and stayed out missed a rebound of about **+51.5%**.

*Source: S&P Dow Jones Indices via FRED (series SP500). Price index, excludes dividends.*

## What this means for you

- A good forecast doesn't protect you on its own. **Timing, the size of your losses, and your emotions** matter just as much.
- A big loss can push you out of the market at the worst moment.
- That's why this module starts with surviving: keeping losses small enough that you can stay in the game.

## Common mistakes

- Judging a trade only by whether your forecast came true.
- Selling in a panic after a fall, then watching the forecast play out.
- Ignoring how a big loss makes the climb back steeper.
