Sometimes everything moves together: stocks, crypto, bonds. Often the cause is not one company or coin, but the economy. Three ideas explain a lot.

## Inflation

**Inflation** is how fast prices across the economy rise. If it's 3% a year, what cost $100 last year costs about $103 now. Your money buys a little less each year.

## Interest rates

A **central bank** (for example the US Federal Reserve or the European Central Bank) sets a key **interest rate**, the price of borrowing money.

- **Higher rates:** borrowing costs more, saving pays more, and spending usually cools. Central banks raise rates to slow inflation.
- **Lower rates:** borrowing is cheaper and spending usually picks up. They're lowered to support growth.

## Why traders care

Higher rates make "safe" savings pay more. So risky assets like growth stocks and crypto have to compete with that. When rates rise quickly, risky assets have often struggled. When rates fall, they've often been helped. "Often" isn't "always".

> **Hypothetical example.** A savings account pays 1%. A risky asset hopes to return 8%. The extra 7% looks worth the risk. If the savings account pays 5%, the extra shrinks to 3%, and some investors move money to safety.

## The dollar

Many assets, including most crypto, oil and gold, are priced in US dollars. When the dollar gets **stronger**, those assets can look cheaper in dollars and often come under pressure. When the dollar weakens, the opposite. The link is loose and changes over time.

## Scheduled events

Big numbers come out on set dates: inflation reports, jobs reports, central bank meetings. Markets often move fast around them because the figure can differ from what was expected. As with earnings, **surprises** move prices, not the numbers themselves.

Typical beginner approach: know the calendar, trade smaller (or not at all) around the big releases, and expect gaps and slippage.

## What not to do

- Don't try to predict the central bank. Professionals get it wrong.
- Don't build a trade on one economic number.
- Don't assume the link between rates and an asset is fixed.

## Common mistakes

- Ignoring scheduled releases and getting hit by a spike.
- Using a theory ("rates up means crypto down") as a certainty.
- Reading only headlines about the economy.

## Check yourself

<details><summary>1. What does inflation measure?</summary><p>How fast prices across the economy are rising.</p></details>

<details><summary>2. Why do central banks raise rates?</summary><p>To cool spending and slow inflation.</p></details>

<details><summary>3. Why might higher rates hurt risky assets?</summary><p>Safe savings pay more, so risky assets must offer more to attract money.</p></details>

<details><summary>4. What moves prices on a data release day?</summary><p>The surprise: the gap between the number and what was expected.</p></details>

<details><summary>5. Is the link between a strong dollar and falling crypto fixed?</summary><p>No. It's loose and changes over time.</p></details>

## Try it (no real money)

Find a public economic calendar. List the next three major releases or central bank meetings, with dates. Note how you'd handle a trade open at that time (smaller, closed, or no change) and why.

**Key idea:** Inflation, interest rates and the dollar move many markets together. Know the calendar and respect the surprises, but don't try to predict them.
